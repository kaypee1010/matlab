The Problem: Will the Runner Cross the Redline?

In Practical 3 you calibrated Unit A's fusion weights so it can turn 3 noisy sensor readings into one trustworthy fused BPM number. That fused BPM is only computed every 20 seconds — the band's processor is small, and running the full sensor fusion continuously would drain the battery in an hour.

A runner wearing Unit A does a graded jog test. Over the first 100 seconds, the band logs 6 fused BPM readings:

Time (s)020406080100
Fused BPM92100110124144172

The runner's coach has set a safety redline: BPM must not exceed 178. If it does, the band should buzz a haptic warning (the motor you'll actually design a control loop for, later in the syllabus) — but the next fused reading isn't due for another 20 seconds. By the time it arrives, the runner may already be well past the redline.

Socho: teen dost (yaani teen sensors) ne mil kar ek number diya — BPM. Lekin wo number sirf har 20 second mein aata hai. Agle 20 second mein kya hoga, ye tumhe abhi, in 6 purane numbers se hi nikaalna hai.

Try it by hand first

Plot the 6 (time, BPM) points on the board. Draw a trend line by eye and extend it forward to guess when — if ever — it crosses 178.

Question to keep in your head: your ruler-and-eye extension took a couple of minutes for 6 points on one runner. The band ships to thousands of runners, each generating a fresh 6-point (or 600-point) history every session. How do you get a trustworthy answer to "will this cross the redline, and when" without redrawing a line by hand every single time?

MEET THE TOOL

polyfit, polyval & roots

polyfit fits the best smooth polynomial curve through noisy data — using all the points at once, not just the last two. polyval evaluates that curve at any new input, past or future. roots tells you exactly where that curve crosses zero (or, with a small shift, any target value like 178).

polyfit se curve banao, polyval se future value nikaalo, roots se "kab cross karega" pata karo — teeno saath mein ek prediction system bante hain.

Tiny example (unrelated to the wearable — just for practice)

x = [1 2 3 4 5];
y = [2.1 3.9 9.2 15.8 25.3];   % looks roughly quadratic

p = polyfit(x, y, 2);   % fit a degree-2 polynomial
y6 = polyval(p, 6);     % evaluate the fitted curve at x = 6

polyfit(x, y, n) returns n+1 coefficients, highest power first — [a b c] means a·x² + b·x + c. roots(p) finds where that polynomial equals zero, e.g. roots([1 -3 2]) gives the roots of x² - 3x + 2.

Quick mental check: what do you think roots([1 -3 2]) will print? Type your guess, then run it in MATLAB to check.
2 and 1

Note: a degree-n fit needs at least n+1 data points to be well-determined. With exactly n+1 points, the curve is forced through every single one — zero averaging, zero noise-cancelling. Keep that in mind for later.

Exactly When Does It Cross the Redline?

Task: Using roots, find the exact time the fitted curve reaches 178 BPM. You'll need to think about how to turn "when does polyval(p2,t) = 178" into "roots of what polynomial equals zero." Discard any root that isn't physically meaningful and explain why in one line. 
p_red = p2;

% ans = -129.68
%        104.88   <- only the positive, future root is physically meaningful

The redline gets crossed at t ≈ 104.9 s — only about 5 seconds after the last real reading came in. The band needs to trigger its haptic warning almost immediately, not wait for the next scheduled 20-second reading.

EXERCISE 4 — Stretch

What If You Trusted a Higher-Degree Fit?

Task: Refit the same 6 points with a degree-5 polynomial instead of degree-2, and predict the BPM at t = 140 again. 
p5 = polyfit(t, bpm, 5);
pred5 = polyval(p5, 140);
disp(pred5)
Before you run it: write one sentence predicting whether this new prediction will be more or less trustworthy than Exercise 2's, and why. 
% pred5 ≈ 260.0 BPM  — even less believable than the degree-2 prediction

With exactly 6 points and 6 coefficients (degree 5), the fit has just enough freedom to thread every single point exactly — including whatever noise was in each reading. It has no averaging power left, so it fits the noise itself, not the underlying trend. Inside t = 0 to 100 it looks perfect; the moment you step outside that range, it's free to swing anywhere. Plot both p2 and p5 together over t = 0 to 140 and look at how differently they behave past t = 100.
